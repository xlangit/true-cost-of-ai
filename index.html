The true cost of an AI token

Water that evaporates, electricity that burns, hardware that ages and land that gets occupied — what you don't see behind every answer.

A token isn't just a number. It's cooling water, kWh from a strained grid, metals from a GPU that will soon be replaced, and capital that isn't profitable yet. This presentation separates the documented from the exaggerated: every figure carries its source.

Why it grows so fast

Token growth and the Jevons paradox

The cost per token falls every year. And yet total consumption rises. It's not a contradiction: it's the Jevons paradox — when something gets more efficient and cheaper, we use it so much more that absolute consumption grows.

The idea that sticks

Reasoning models generate hundreds to thousands of hidden "thinking tokens" before answering — that's why they cost 5–10× more and emit far more. The "smarter" the answer, the more physical resources behind it.

⚡ Electricity & CO₂

The electricity that burns

Not all queries cost the same. An empirical test on real GPUs shows a two-orders-of-magnitude difference between a small model and a large reasoner.

CO₂ per question — measured on real GPUs

Both figures in grams, to compare them at a glance:

Qwen 7B small model
27.7 g
DeepSeek-R1 70B reasoner
~2,000 g

In plain terms: 27.7 g is like leaving an LED bulb on for ~20 minutes. ~2,000 g (that is, 2 kg) is about 75 bulbs for the same time — or driving a car about 10 km. The reasoner emits almost 75× more in this case, because it generates thousands of hidden "thinking tokens" before answering.

The global aggregate

The IEA projects data centers go from 415 TWh (1.5% of all the world's electricity, 2024) to ~945 TWh in 2030 — growing ~12% a year, more than four times faster than total electricity demand. In plain terms: today they consume as much as all of France in a year; by 2030, almost as much as all of Japan. The IEA names AI as "the most important factor in that growth".

Sources Dauner & Socher — Frontiers in Communication (2025) · IEA — Energy & AI (2025)
🖥️ Hardware

The hardware that ages

GPUs aren't retired because they fail, but because the next generation performs better. That accelerated cycle feeds one of the planet's fastest-growing waste streams.

of e-waste globally in 2022 — only 22.3% formally recycled

A tonne of circuit boards contains on the order of 90 g of gold, 400 g of silver and 200 kg of copper — concentrations far above mine rock. That's why e-waste is called an "urban mine": throwing out hardware is throwing out high-grade metal.

🏭 Physical space

The land and grid that get occupied

You can't build a data center just anywhere: it needs land, water and — above all — a power grid that can take it. Those limits are stricter than the "infinite scale" narrative suggests.

176 TWh
US data-center consumption (2023) = 4.4% of national electricity (LBNL)
325–580 TWh
2028 projection = 6.7–12% of the national total
tens of GW
feasible US hyperscale capacity — "not hundreds" (Janosov, 2026)
Why this matters

Here's the key tension: demand projections for AI data centers run into the hundreds of gigawatts. But because of grid, water and suitable-land limits, only tens realistically fit in the US. That gap — hundreds requested, tens possible — is AI's real physical ceiling: either demand goes unmet, or it spills over to other countries, or it ends up straining everyone's power grid.

Sources Janosov (2026) — Feasibility of US hyperscale siting · LBNL — US Data Center Energy Usage (2024)
⚖️ The backlash

When the data center comes to the neighborhood

The limit isn't only technical — it's social and political. As data centers move closer to homes, complaints about noise and emissions grow, states start to legislate, and the power to feed them — including the nuclear bet — arrives slower than demand.

🔊 Noise: a hum that never stops

Cooling systems (fans, chillers) produce a constant low-frequency hum. In Loudoun County (Virginia), "Data Center Alley", up to 90 dB was measured near a facility — the residential limit is 55 dB. In Chandler (Arizona), after years of complaints, the city council rejected a $2.5 billion AI data center 7-0 (Dec 2025). In Granbury (Texas), residents sued over a ~90 dB noise they link to headaches, tinnitus and high blood pressure.

💨 Emissions: gas turbines next to homes

The flagship case: xAI's "Colossus" supercomputer in Memphis ran dozens of unpermitted gas turbines next to majority-Black neighborhoods. The EPA found Clean Air Act violations (Jan 2026) and the NAACP sued (Apr 2026); emissions include NOx, fine particulates and ~19 tonnes/year of formaldehyde (a carcinogen). In Virginia, thousands of diesel backup generators raise air-quality concerns.

🏛️ The law reacts

~267 bills
on data centers across 40+ US states in 2025 (~40 enacted); 300+ more in the first weeks of 2026
7-0
votes by which Tucson (AZ) rejected "Project Blue" and Chandler its AI data center
moratoriums

Texas passed SB 6 (a "kill switch": large loads must be disconnectable during grid emergencies); Loudoun ended by-right construction. Not everything passes — many bills die in committee — but the pushback is real and bipartisan.

☢️ The nuclear bet that hasn't arrived

To power this 24/7 demand without coal, Big Tech turned back to nuclear: Microsoft is restarting Three Mile Island (835 MW), Amazon signed with Talen (up to 1.92 GW), Google with Kairos (500 MW by 2035) and Meta up to 6.6 GW.

The uncomfortable detail Big Tech has signed more than 10 GW of nuclear power in a year — but as of mid-2026, ~0 GW of new or restarted nuclear is actually delivering power to a data center. Restarts take years (Three Mile Island ~2027, Duane Arnold ~2029) and no commercial small modular reactor (SMR) operates yet in the US. The power exists in the announcements; on the grid, it arrives around 2027–2030. The demand, meanwhile, is already here.
Sources Stateline — state moratoriums & laws · Earthjustice — xAI's Memphis turbines · Constellation — Three Mile Island restart
🌍 Not just the US

Europe and Japan: two different models

The US builds first and regulates later. Europe does the opposite, and Japan serves as a yardstick. Three views that show the problem is global.

🇮🇪 Ireland — the extreme case

Data centers already consume 22% of the country's entire electricity (2024) — more than all urban households combined. Projected at ~30% by 2032.

🇪🇺 Europe — regulates first

Mandatory energy and water reporting, and a brake: Ireland froze new Dublin grid connections (2021–2025); now they're only allowed if the data center generates its own power.

🇯🇵 Japan — the yardstick

By 2030, the world's data centers will consume almost as much as all of Japan today. And Japan itself triples its own consumption by 2034 — with 40%+ of the new power from coal and gas.

The idea that sticks

Ireland shows where the "build without limits" model leads: nearly a quarter of a developed country's electricity dedicated to server farms. Europe responded by tying each new data center to bringing its own power — a brake the US still doesn't have.

Sources Irish Times / CSO — Ireland 22% · Wood Mackenzie — Japan triples by 2034 · IEA — 945 TWh ≈ Japan
❄️ Cooling

The cold that's no longer optional

A modern AI rack (NVIDIA GB200) can draw 120 kW, and almost all of that energy ends up as heat. Here's what isn't obvious: a huge share of a data center's electricity — and of its investment — doesn't power the chips, it's spent just cooling them. At this density air no longer cuts it, and liquid cooling went from luxury to requirement.

First, understanding PUE

PUE (power usage effectiveness) measures how much electricity goes to everything that isn't compute — mostly cooling. A PUE of 1.0 would be perfect; every tenth above that is wasted energy.

Industry average conventional air
1.54
Hyperscalers Google, Meta… (optimized air)
~1.10
Liquid cooling the target
<1.2

In plain terms: a PUE of 1.54 means that for every watt reaching the chips, another half-watt is spent just on cooling and losses — roughly a third of all the data center's electricity goes to moving heat, not computing. And it's not only energy: cooling equipment is also a giant share of the cost to build the data center. That's why lowering PUE with liquid cooling is where much of the efficiency battle is fought.

Who makes the cold? The West leads, China is rising fast

🇺🇸🇪🇺 The market leaders are Western

Vertiv (US) is the clear leader — its liquid business more than doubled revenue in 2025. Behind it: Schneider Electric (France, bought Motivair), CoolIT (Canada), LiquidStack, Boyd and nVent (US). It's not a China-dominated industry.

🇨🇳 China is growing from its own demand

Envicool (Shenzhen, +40% revenue), Huawei and Inspur are gaining ground on cost and volume. The signal: in March 2026 Google entered talks to buy from Envicool — Chinese suppliers now credible even to a US hyperscaler, but competing with Vertiv, not displacing it.

Japan and Europe contribute too, especially in immersion cooling: Fujitsu and NEC/KDDI (Japan, up to ~94% less cooling energy in trials), Submer (Spain) and Iceotope (UK).

A concrete example Haier (China) unveiled a solution for AI data centers with active magnetic-levitation bearings that takes up to 70% less space and keeps PUE below 1.2 — versus the 1.54 industry average. The liquid-cooling market for AI servers goes from ~$8.9 billion (2025) to over $17 billion (2026), per JPMorgan.
Sources Uptime Institute — PUE 1.54 & cooling 2025 · Reuters — Google, Envicool & JPMorgan market · China Daily — Haier AIDC

The circular money machine

Part of AI's "growth" is circular: the same companies fund and buy from each other. Nvidia commits to investing up to $100 billion in OpenAI; OpenAI signs a $300 billion cloud deal with Oracle and commits to deploying 6 GW of AMD chips (with an option to buy 160 million AMD shares); Oracle, in turn, spends tens of billions on Nvidia chips. The money returns to where it started.

Why it worries — and why it isn't "fraud" Analysts at Bernstein and the Financial Times warn this circularity echoes the "vendor financing" of the dot-com bubble (Nortel, Lucent): if the same companies buy from each other, how much demand is real? The solid proof of genuine demand is revenue that comes from outside the circle. Honest nuance: these are commitments and letters of intent that play out over years, not cash already spent — a legitimate concern, not illegality.
"OpenAI's business model is not sustainable." — Kai-Fu Lee, CEO of 01.AI (Bloomberg, March 2025)
Common myths about an AI "financial collapse"

Alarmist figures circulate that don't survive a check against primary sources:

  • ❌ "The SEC is investigating $5.7 billion for wash trading" and "testimony from a Microsoft whistleblower" → don't exist in any credible source.
  • ❌ "7-month runway (OpenAI) / 5.3 months (Anthropic)" → unsupported; both are funded far beyond that.
  • ❌ "45% drop in AI funding" → it's the opposite: Q1 2026 was a record (~$255 billion, PitchBook).
  • ❌ "68% of revenue is cloud credits" → a mix-up with a "+68% in inference costs".

What is real: low margins, huge cash burn and a genuine debate about profitability. The honest conclusion isn't "collapse", but a capital bet not yet resolved.

Sources Sacra — OpenAI margins & compute · Fortune — OpenAI's confidential S-1 · PitchBook — record Q1 2026 funding
🇨🇳 The China factor

Cheap tokens: efficiency or subsidy?

China sells tokens far cheaper than the US — sometimes tens of times cheaper. It's worth understanding why, because the answer mixes real ingenuity, cheap energy and subsidies — and it doesn't mean they've solved the underlying problem.

3–6× … up to 80×
cheaper Chinese tokens: ~3–6× at the high end and up to ~80× on the budget models (DeepSeek vs Claude Opus)
~3¢ vs 7–9¢
per kWh for data centers in China vs the US — structurally cheaper energy
~60%
how well China's best chip (Huawei Ascend) performs against an Nvidia H100 in inference — its bottleneck

Why so cheap? Four reasons, not one

✅ Real efficiency

DeepSeek redesigned the model's "memory" (sparse MoE + MLA) and cut compute per query by up to ~93%. Validated by independent analysis, not marketing.

✅ Price war

In 2024 ByteDance launched Doubao at a rock-bottom price and, the same week, Alibaba, Baidu and Tencent cut theirs. A domestic battle for share drove prices to the floor.

⚠️ Cheap energy + subsidies

Local governments subsidize up to 50% of a data center's power bill — but only if it uses Chinese chips, not Nvidia. A push with political strings attached.

📉 Global trend

It's not just China: the inference cost of a GPT-3.5-class model fell ~280× in 18 months across the whole industry (Stanford HAI).

Do they make money? No — just like the US No Chinese AI company is verifiably profitable on its own. DeepSeek's famous "545% margin" is a theoretical figure from the company itself (it assumes 100% paid traffic at peak price). The giants subsidize their AI: Alibaba's operating profit fell 84%, partly from spending to acquire users for Qwen.
Can you trust the Chinese data? With caveats. Regulated filings (SEC, Hong Kong exchange) are solid. Self-reported claims ("we trained for $5.6 million") should be taken with a grain of salt: that number is only the compute for one training run; total infrastructure is around $1.6 billion. And Chinese government statistics have documented low credibility. That said: opacity has grown across the whole industry, not just in China.
Do they face the same physical problems? Energy yes, chips worse
  • Energy: less of a problem. Cheaper, more abundant electricity; China added ~8× more generation capacity than the US in 2024. (But ~70% of its AI compute still runs on coal.)
  • Water: similar. ~1.3 billion m³/year and ~40% of its racks in drought-prone areas — a situation similar to the US.
  • Chips: worse. Export controls cut them off from the best Nvidia. The 2025–2026 irony: after the back-and-forth, it's Beijing that now blocks Nvidia chips to force the use of Huawei — which performs ~60% of an H100 and uses ~4× more power.
Sources DeepSeek — official pricing · SemiAnalysis — DeepSeek's real costs · Stanford HAI — ~280× drop in inference cost
📈 The moment of truth

When belief turns into cash

For years AI rose on belief: investors convinced it would be huge. In 2026 the test arrives — the biggest AI companies hit the public markets almost at once, and that demands real, new money, not just enthusiasm.

SpaceX · $75B
went public June 12, 2026 (already merged with xAI) — the largest IPO in history, ~$2.1 trillion market cap
Anthropic · OpenAI
filed confidential S-1s (June 1 & 8); private valuations ~$965B and ~$852B; IPO possible this fall
Google · $85B*
announced the largest stock sale in its history (June 2026). *But only ~$45B has actually closed; the remaining ~$40B is sold gradually — and largely for employee-equity taxes, not data centers.

Why it matters: new money, not screen prices

When a stock rises on the market, no new money goes in — only what already exists is repriced. An IPO or a stock issuance is different: someone has to put up real cash to buy all those new shares. And the sum is enormous: the biggest IPO year in history (2021) raised ~$142 billion; 2025 raised ~$46 billion. SpaceX + OpenAI + Anthropic + Google alone add up to far more than that.

Google's move

Google is already public, profitable and has many income sources. So why announce the largest stock sale in its history (up to $85B; ~$45B already closed)? Analysts (Gil Luria, DA Davidson) read it this way: get in first to capture the capital before SpaceX, Anthropic and OpenAI absorb it — benefiting from the boom while hedging against it ("if AI disappoints, we've still got YouTube"). That's the market's interpretation, not Google's official reason, which is funding infrastructure.

Why this is the most delicate moment A bubble doesn't burst because the technology is fake, but when the cost of keeping it inflated exceeds confidence. Until now AI rose on belief; now it asks for hundreds of billions in cash all at once. If investors hesitate — "too expensive, I'll wait six months" — confidence cools. It's not saying AI is worthless; it's saying "not at that price, not yet."
Sources CNBC — Alphabet's ~$85B offering · CNBC — SpaceX record IPO · Fortune — Anthropic's S-1 ($965B)
Synthesis

What to measure before deciding

The question isn't "is AI useful?" but "do we know its real cost?". After separating the documented from the exaggerated, three firm truths remain:

1 · The physical cost is real and growing

Water, electricity (415→945 TWh), e-waste and land are well documented by the IEA, AI2, LBNL and UNITAR. It's not alarmism: it's measurement.

2 · Jevons rules

Every efficiency gain is swallowed — and exceeded — by more usage. Absolute consumption rises even as cost per token falls.

3 · The economics aren't settled

Low margins and huge burn — but record capital, not collapse. It's an open bet, not an announced bankruptcy.

If we don't measure the physical and financial cost with real data — not made-up figures — we won't know whether the result is worth the resource. That's the only honest conclusion.
